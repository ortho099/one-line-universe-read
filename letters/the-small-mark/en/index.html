The Mark That Stayed

Not every dark speck needs rubbing out.

We held the mirror handle until both our hands began to ache. Only then did the little figure on the page turn his head straight again. The cloth slipped a little, without revealing his feet. A black dot had appeared inside the circle he was holding. I let go of my half of the handle; it had pressed a red line into my palm. My son changed hands to support it, without asking me to keep helping. I set the handle down securely before looking at the diary again.

The dot was beside half a face. An ear, an eye, a scrap of graphite—I couldn't tell. I tilted the real mirror toward the lamp. All I could see was a greasy streak on my glasses.

“How can you see anything in a mirror this dirty?” I said.

My son looked at me. “When it was showing you a moment ago, you said it was perfectly clear.”

I wiped it with the hem of my shirt. The streak grew wider without getting cleaner. He took the mirror and laid it back on the table, out of reach of my polishing. The dot on the page, meanwhile, was being drawn darker. The little figure pointed to it with his other hand.

I reached for the eraser.

“Not his drawing. I just want to clear the crumbs beside it,” I said. Even to me, it sounded rather like something I used to say before correcting his homework.

He raised a hand, palm toward me, without touching my wrist. The eraser hovered above the paper. Suddenly I wasn't sure which little speck I had meant to rub away.

He picked up a tiny scrap of graphite from the edge of the receipt, placed it on an undrawn part of the back, and tilted the paper. The black crumb fell onto the table. The dot in the diary stayed inside its circle.

“This one falls off,” he said. “That one might not be a crumb.”

I put the eraser back at the edge of the table. He returned the receipt beneath the tracing paper at its original angle. We hadn't moved the tall chair, the low chair or the third, empty cup. No one had touched the white saucer by the door again. The tracing paper lay flat; the slipper beside the drawn chair leg was down to a few faint lines. I didn't lift it for another look, afraid a change of light would make me misread it yet again.

I asked whether he remembered testing me on a Chinese question years ago. He thought for a moment. There had been so many, he said; he didn't know which one I'd got wrong. I picked up my phone and found the old post, 〈仔仔教中文：幾時用「今．令」〉.

It was so short it didn't fill a screen. His younger self had asked me when those two characters had a dot and when they didn't, then explained it in terms of an extra sound at the end of their pronunciation. I read the title aloud and almost wrote it beside the diary drawing without thinking.

The pen didn't touch the page. Today's one line was still my question about what year it was on the other side. After what felt like a whole evening of events, I hadn't gained even a single word. I moved the pen toward the ordinary receipt, then stopped again, afraid of pressing through the tracing paper above it.

“So you really didn't know,” my son said.

“I said I didn't know back then, too.”

He glanced at the phone and laughed. “Then we haven't only just discovered it.”

I remembered expecting a long explanation from him. In the end, the answer had come down to one dot. Tonight, faced with another tiny black mark, my first impulse had been to clear it away. The phone screen went dark. I put it behind my cup and left the eraser alone.

The little figure in the diary reached his free hand to the edge of the circle. His fingertip stopped beside the dot. A small patch of graphite grew faint, then was drawn in again. Half the finger was rubbed out as well; when redrawn, it was shorter.

“He's erasing it,” I said.

My son leaned down until his eyes were almost level with the table.

“Look at which part is getting lighter.”

I looked for a long time. It was the cheek line beside the dot that had been rubbed away. The dot was still there. The little figure stopped turning his head and tilted the circle toward his own side instead. The half-face slowly slipped out of the circle, leaving a tuft of untidy hair.

Then the hair disappeared too.

· · ·

I lifted the real mirror, trying to copy his angle. As I turned it, the lamp flashed into my son's eyes and he flinched. I tipped it down at once. My knees appeared in the glass.

“Don't turn it for him just yet,” he said.

I propped the mirror against a cup. It reflected no one's face and wasn't facing the door. A small patch of warm light fell on the table. The circle on the page was still in the little figure's hand; my knees hadn't appeared in it. I edged my tall chair back a little and drew my feet beneath it. The low chair beside me was still empty, its circular stain in the same pool of lamplight.

A short line appeared below the dot in the circle. After a while, two long sides joined it, making a shape with a rounded end. At first I took it for a pencil. Only when I couldn't find the point did I notice the edge of a fingernail at the top.

A finger. Two lines wrapped across its middle. The black dot sat on the edge of one of them, with a little corner curling up.

I looked at my own index finger. The collar had caught my plaster, and its corner still curled back, refusing to stay flat no matter how I pressed it. I'd rubbed it with my thumb several times. A dark thread from my clothes was stuck to the adhesive edge.

“Does it look like it?” I asked.

My son didn't say yes, or pull my hand nearer the paper. He pointed to the window higher up in the diary drawing.

The thing resembling a comb withdrew halfway behind the window. The large hand remained. Its thumb loosened and the long finger that had been bent straightened a little. Just below the knuckle, two short lines came into view.

I didn't look at our door. I looked between those two lines, as though paying enough attention might let me recognise which comb a hand had once held.

My son moved his cup aside, clearing a little space on the table.

“Put your hand here for now.”

I laid it down, palm first. It wasn't touching the diary; several inches separated it from the edge of the page. The hand behind the window didn't follow. It stayed upright behind the glass. One hand lay flat, the other bent; even their fingertips pointed in different directions.

I waited for him to say it wasn't me. Instead, he moved the lamp farther away so the real plaster wouldn't catch the light.

The little figure turned the circle, drawing its mirror surface facing us. The section of finger remained inside it. Another tiny corner appeared beside the black dot. The door stayed shut, and the large hand didn't cross the window frame.

I thought of the real plaster that had fallen out of the diary. A corner of its wrapper had been folded, with graphite crumbs stuck to it. I no longer remembered where those crumbs were. I hadn't even kept the wrapper properly.

I didn't peel off the plaster on my hand. With a corner of the receipt, my son carefully scooped up the graphite crumb that had fallen onto the table. He folded both sides of the paper up and put it beside the third, empty cup.

“Let's keep it this time,” he said.

I nodded. My palm was still against the table, but my thumb drifted toward my index finger, wanting to press that curled corner flat. Halfway there, I stopped.

The dot on the page hadn't moved.
