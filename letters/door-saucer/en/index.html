The Saucer by the Door

They hear the same sound coming from different places.

The half beat passed. My hand was still in the air. My son pointed at my knee and I remembered to put it down. The tuft of dust under the little chair hadn’t scattered. There was nothing new in the seam between the tiles by its leg.

I wanted to ask him how many sounds he’d heard. He pushed the chopsticks to the middle of the table, well beyond my reach, before he spoke.

“Where did you hear the last one?”

“Under the chair. You?”

He pointed towards the front door. We’d been crouching on either side of the chair, a few floor tiles apart. He’d also heard a faint scuff after the last tap, as if a sole had brushed the floor. I hadn’t heard that part.

We didn’t carry the chair over to the door and try again. He held the edge of the table and stood up. His knee popped. I laughed at him for making that sound at his age. Then I stood up and both of mine popped. He turned round and held up two fingers.

The tracing paper was still flat on the table. Next to the third cup there was only one of the child’s shoes. A moment ago I’d been able to see half the sole of the other one. Now there were just a few fine lines. Thinking the paper had folded, I lifted the receipt underneath and tilted it towards the light. The missing patch stayed white.

My son pointed to the remaining shoe with the end of his pencil. There was a grey blur in front of its toe. The edges slowly sharpened. I leaned in until my nose almost touched the paper. I thought I could see a crown with three points.

“Clown,” my son said.

I looked at that corner of the paper and remembered coming home late one evening to find a pair of clown slippers by the door. I’d heard crown when my son said clown. He had to say it again before I could hear the difference. I remembered how to say both words after that. I still couldn’t tell you whose slippers they were.

“Do you remember those slippers?” I asked.

“I remember you misheard me,” he said. “The slippers, no.”

He turned the tracing paper sideways and pointed out the three tips. The middle one was a hat. The ones on either side were tufts of hair. Below them was a nose so small and round I would have taken it for a speck of pencil lead if I’d blinked.

The shoe on the paper had had laces. This one had none. The opening was wide enough to slip a foot straight into. There was no foot drawn on the chair. The clown slipper lay beside its leg with the toe pointing away from the table.

I looked down at the floor. There was still nothing under the real chair. Nobody had so much as swept away that tuft of dust. I nearly told my son, “He took his shoe off.” I pressed my tongue against my top teeth and swallowed the words.

I opened the diary. The door was still shut and the little figure was still crouching beside it. The thing he’d held against the bottom of the door was in front of his knees now. It had looked like a bowl tipped on its side. Seen from above, with its shallow rim, it looked like a saucer.

He drew two drops of water. The first landed in the saucer. The second hung at the bottom of the door, its tip touching the seam. I waited for him to draw a third. He drew the second drop into the saucer with a short line. Then he stopped.

· · ·

“Where’s our umbrella?” my son asked.

The hook by the door was empty. The umbrella had dripped on the floor earlier. I’d moved it over the bathtub and wiped the tiles with an old towel. I told him so. He felt the tile by the door with the back of his hand and turned his palm over to show me. There was no water.

We kept our keys in a white saucer on top of the shoe cabinet. He tipped my keys into his palm, set them back on the cabinet and crouched with the empty saucer.

“Move it in a bit,” I said. “Someone will kick it and break it if we open the door.”

He glanced at me. I remembered we hadn’t meant to open the door all evening. He drew the saucer in a little anyway, to the edge of the mat. There was just enough room for a flat hand between it and the door.

I was going to fetch the diary and bring it over to compare. He asked me for a paper towel. I tore off two sheets in the kitchen. The kitchen light was bright enough to hurt my eyes. I only noticed that a corner of my bandage had lifted after I switched it off. When I came back he was still crouching in the same spot, facing the door.

“See anything?”

“I was waiting for you.”

The sheets didn’t quite line up. I straightened them as I crouched down. My son put his hand on the back of mine. There was a faint curve on my side of the white saucer. In the lamplight I thought it was a scratch in the glaze. I moved my head to the other side. The curve was still there. Slowly, it grew thicker.

He took the top sheet and touched a corner to the curve. It soaked up a little water, a spot smaller than a fingernail. The underside of the saucer was dry. So was the mat. I looked up at the top of the door. The paint was clean. My son looked under the shoe cabinet. Neither of us had seen a drop fall.

He spread the sheet on my knee. The wet spot was almost transparent. I waited for him to say it was left from washing the saucer, or that I hadn’t wiped the floor properly. He only asked if I still had another sheet.

I put the second sheet on the other side of the mat. He held the saucer from underneath and turned the wet edge towards himself. We waited. No new spots appeared in the saucer and the fresh sheet didn’t change colour. From the bathroom came a single, unmistakable drip in the tub.

We went back to the table. The slipper was still there on the tracing paper. There was a curved line under the round nose now. I would probably have said that the clown was starting to smile. This time I looked for the eyes first. Neither had been drawn.

My son pushed the diary towards me. The little figure had sat down beside the door. The saucer was still in front of his feet. I drew a paper towel and added the little wet spot in the corner. I’d meant to add a question mark. My pencil hung there for a moment, then moved across to draw a second sheet, clean and dry.

The third cup on the table was still empty. My son took it by the handle and asked if I wanted some warm water. I put the chopsticks back in the drawer. Yes, I said. Two cups would do.

While he was in the kitchen, there was another sound from the diary. Very soft, like the end of a pencil falling against the page. I didn’t call him back. On the page, the little figure drew the two sheets together. They were stacked just like the ones in my hand, with a little corner sticking out on the left.

With my finger I eased my two sheets a little apart. The saucer was still by the door. I turned to look. When I looked back, there was a small gap on the page, too.
