Lower a Little

A mirror held too high can leave you unable to see.

My son came back carrying two cups of warm water. He saw the paper towels pinched between my fingers. “Are you going to tear them again?”

I loosened my grip. The gap between the two sheets was there in the diary too. I wanted to pull them a little further apart. He put down the cups first and asked if I’d watched them the whole time.

“I looked over at the door.”

He moved his cup a little further away. He didn’t say the gap must have changed because of me. I laid the towels flat. The wet spot was beginning to dry, and the corner felt stiffer than the rest.

The table lamp was warming half my face. I loosened my collar. The bandage on my index finger caught on a button, and its lifted corner wouldn’t stick back down. My son saw it and pointed at his head.

“You used to catch me like that when you cut my hair.”

I remembered. With a bandage on, I couldn’t quite feel the comb. When I put the comb down, the bandage stuck to the towel. I told him he had at least sat still back then. He touched the back of his ear. “Because you said you’d cut my ears off if I moved.”

I might have said something, though surely nothing that severe. I found the old blog post on my phone. There I was, saying I’d learnt to cut the sides but still made a mess of the middle. I’d even claimed to be eighty per cent as good as a professional.

“The other twenty per cent was on my head,” he said.

I turned the phone face down before he could read any more. Laughing, he went to the drawer and came back with a small round hand mirror. There was old tape at its edge. The handle had worked loose years ago. I’d taped it up and left it at that.

I used to hold it so he could see how I’d cut the back. He kept turning his head. The more he turned, the less he could see, and I told him to sit straight. All that time I’d been telling him to look directly at a place he needed two mirrors to see.

Now he set the mirror on the table, facing the tracing paper. “Hold the lamp for me.”

I lifted the lamp until its cable was taut. He said it didn’t need to be any higher, just further to the side. I thought he wanted to see the back of the paper in the mirror. After a while I couldn’t help asking what he could see.

“Your hand. It’s blocking everything.”

I drew back to my left and the hand in the mirror went right. The figure with glasses on the tracing paper raised a hand too, but I hadn’t noticed which side. By the time I tried to look, my son was asking me to steady the lamp first.

The sideways light picked out the pencil marks from our earlier tracings, one by one. Dark on the creases, pale where the paper was flat. The circle that had looked like a clown’s nose was still beside the chair leg. A tuft of lines stuck out above it.

He gently propped up the corner of the sheet with the back of the mirror. He didn’t turn the diary over. With the light coming from the side, I could finally see a few short lines beside the third cup, close to the back of the chair.

I thought they were more pencil crumbs and reached to brush them away. My son brought the mirror handle between them and my hand, then bent to look.

“They’re in the paper.”

I picked up the diary and tilted it the same way. The little figure was still sitting beside the door. The saucer in front of his knees had moved aside. There was a row of short, uneven lines on top of his head now. One stuck out at an angle.

“Hair?” I asked.

My son swept the tuft on his own forehead to one side. I couldn’t tell whether he was demonstrating or whether leaning down for so long had got it in his eyes. The drawing in the diary didn’t immediately change.

The little figure reached for something broad. At first I took it for a paper towel. He wrapped the two ends round his neck and let the front hang past his knees. The space where his shoes would have been stayed blank.

I looked at my son. He looked at me. Neither of us spoke first this time. I remembered the towel I used to fasten behind his neck for a haircut. He’d say it itched, and I’d tell him to bear it a bit longer.

A hand came out from under the cloth on the page, palm up, as if waiting to be given something. The other hand held a circle with a short handle. I raised the mirror in front of me and one of my own eyes appeared in it.

“What do you think he’s doing?” I asked.

“Let him finish drawing first.”

My son took the mirror from me by the handle and slowly turned it towards me. He sat on the other side of the lamp. I could see my temple in the glass. My hair was messier than I’d thought, with a tuft sticking up behind the arm of my glasses.

I didn’t go looking for scissors. In the old days, whenever I decided the sides weren’t even, I’d cut them shorter and shorter. I pulled the diary closer and drew a comb in an unused corner. One long edge, a few teeth. It didn’t need to be beautiful.

The little figure on the other side raised his circle and drew two ears and half a face inside it. Then he stopped. Behind the window in the drawn door, the large hand appeared again, finger by finger.

My throat tightened. I looked at our front door. The white saucer was still by the mat. The paper towel beside it hadn’t moved. My son tapped the tabletop to bring my eyes back to the diary.

The hand behind the window bent two fingers. Its thumb pinched a fine line. The end of the line split in two, and my first thought was scissors. But there was no pivot between the two lines and no finger loops beneath them. I looked again. One of the lines had acquired a few teeth.

The comb I’d drawn was still in my own corner.

“Is he holding a comb?” I said.

“Looks like it.”

My hand was already on the edge of the diary. I was about to draw the little figure a taller chair. My son didn’t stop me. Instead, he asked why I’d always held the mirror higher and higher when I showed him the back of his hair.

Because I’d been standing, I said. Then I looked down. On the page, the little figure had both hands up beside his ears. The circle was so high it showed only the upper half of his face.

I erased the chair legs I’d begun and drew a hand held a little lower instead. I didn’t know whether anyone on the other side would understand, or whether that cloth really was for a haircut. I put down the pencil when I’d finished.

My son set the little mirror at the edge of the table, face up. He took a sip of water and found a comb to smooth his fringe. I teased him for still caring about that missing twenty per cent. He said my bandage was stuck to my collar right now, so I ought to sort myself out first.

With my other hand I peeled up the bandage corner and freed my collar. The circle in the diary slowly came down too. It stopped in front of the little figure’s chest. A short, jagged edge appeared inside it.

Like the uneven tuft on top of his head.

The door was still shut. The hand behind the window hadn’t reached out. Facing his circle, the little figure drew a hand on the back of his own head and slowly lowered his head.

The real mirror was reflecting the lamp. Nobody was looking into it. I reached over and turned it towards my son. He glanced at it, then reached out to help me hold it.

Each of us held half the handle and waited for the head on the paper to finish turning.
